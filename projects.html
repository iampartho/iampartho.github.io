Project 01 · Aim 1 · Ongoing

Train on CT, segment MRI

Can a lung segmentation model that learned from CT work on an MRI it has never seen?

Ultrashort-echo-time (UTE) MRI images the lungs without ionizing radiation, which makes it ideal for children and for repeated scans. But labeled UTE MRI data is scarce, and a model trained on CT usually breaks down on MRI because the two modalities look completely different.

In our ISBI 2025 paper, training jointly on CT and a small set of labeled MRI already improved UTE MRI segmentation. I’m now going after the harder setting: reaching strong MRI accuracy with a model trained only on CT, by teaching it to rely on lung shape rather than scanner-specific brightness, and by handling the shift to MRI without any MRI labels.

Why it mattersRadiation-free lung imaging only becomes quantitative when segmentation is reliable. Removing the need for MRI labels makes that possible on any scanner, at any site.
  • UTE MRI
  • CT
  • Domain generalization
  • 3D segmentation

Project 02 · Aim 2 · Ongoing

MRI → CT synthesis that respects anatomy

Turn a radiation-free MRI into a CT-like image without moving a single lung boundary.

Decades of mature analysis tools exist for lung CT. If a UTE MRI could be translated into a realistic CT-like image, all of those tools could run on MRI directly. Paired MRI and CT scans of the same patient are rare, so I work with unpaired image translation (CycleGAN-style models).

The catch is that these models are great at making images look realistic, but they can quietly change the anatomy, shrinking or inflating the lungs. My work focuses on constraining the translation so the anatomy is preserved by design, not by luck.

Why it mattersA synthetic CT you can trust anatomically lets proven CT pipelines for segmentation and quantitative biomarkers extend to radiation-free MRI.
  • Unpaired translation
  • GANs
  • Anatomy preservation
  • UTE MRI → CT

Project 03 · Aim 3 · Ongoing since Nov 2024

A foundation model for the lung

Learn the lung once from unlabeled scans, then reuse that knowledge wherever labels are scarce.

General-purpose medical models exist, but surprisingly few are built specifically for the lung, even though it is a self-contained organ with many clinical tasks. I’m investigating self-supervised pretraining strategies that let a model learn lung anatomy from large collections of unlabeled scans.

The second half of the question is adaptation: how to fine-tune such a model for new modalities and tasks, such as UTE MRI, from only a handful of labeled examples without forgetting what it learned during pretraining.

Why it mattersOne strong pretrained lung model could make every downstream task work in the low-data regime, from lung and airway segmentation to disease characterization.
  • Self-supervised learning
  • Foundation models
  • Representation learning
  • Low-data adaptation

Project 04 · Ongoing since Feb 2024

Airways in fibrotic lungs

Segmenting the airway tree where disease makes it hardest to see.

Airway segmentation underpins the assessment of COPD, fibrosis and lung cancer, and guides navigation bronchoscopy, yet tracing an airway tree by hand can take around 15 hours per scan. In fibrotic lung disease it gets harder still: honeycomb cysts look strikingly like airway lumens on CT, so networks “leak” into them, and even expert labels miss fine branches.

I’m building full-resolution 3D segmentation that holds up in these lungs, trained on carefully curated scans from multiple public challenge datasets.

Why it mattersReliable airway trees in diseased lungs are a prerequisite for measuring airway remodeling and for planning interventions.
  • Airway segmentation
  • Fibrotic lung disease
  • 3D U-Net
  • Full-resolution CT
